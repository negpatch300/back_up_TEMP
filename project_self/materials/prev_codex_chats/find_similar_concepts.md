Concepts in the same family as abstraction, perspective, and context include:

Framing: How something is presented or bounded so certain meanings stand out.
Modeling: Creating a simplified representation of something complex.
Interpretation: The meaning someone gives to information or experience.
Lens: A way of seeing or analyzing something, like “economic lens” or “ethical lens.”
Scope: What is included or excluded from consideration.
Granularity: The level of detail you are working at.
Layering: Understanding something through different levels, from surface to deep structure.
Representation: How something is expressed, encoded, or depicted.
Viewpoint: A position from which something is understood.
Frame of reference: The assumptions, position, or system used to make sense of something.
Paradigm: A larger worldview or model that shapes thinking.
Assumption: Something treated as true in order to reason further.
Mental model: An internal structure for understanding how something works.
Ontology: What kinds of things are assumed to exist in a system or theory.
Categorization: Grouping things according to shared traits.
Generalization: Moving from specific cases to broader patterns.
Simplification: Reducing complexity to make something easier to reason about.
Salience: What stands out as important within a situation.
Interpretive frame: The background structure that shapes how something is understood.
Situatedness: The idea that understanding depends on one’s position, environment, history, or role.
A compact cluster would be:

abstraction, framing, perspective, context, scope, granularity, representation, interpretation, mental model, paradigm, frame of reference.

Yes, with that context, you’re pointing at meta-concepts for shaping, selecting, transforming, or interpreting data.

Here’s a useful map.

Forms Or Containers Of Data

Context: surrounding data that gives meaning to some focal data.
Metadata: data about data, like author, time, source, format, tags.
Schema: the structure that says what kinds of data are allowed.
Model: a representation of data and relationships.
Frame: a bounded way of organizing data for interpretation.
State: the current values of data at a moment.
Trace: a record of how data or events unfolded over time.
Provenance: where data came from and how it changed.
Namespace: a named container that prevents ambiguity.
Domain: the world or subject area the data belongs to.
Operations On Data

Abstraction: removing detail to keep only what matters at a chosen level.
Filtering: selecting only some data.
Scoping: defining what data is visible or relevant.
Projection: selecting certain fields or dimensions from data.
Aggregation: combining many pieces of data into summaries.
Normalization: reshaping data into a consistent form.
Encoding: turning data into a representation.
Decoding: interpreting encoded data back into meaning.
Transformation: changing data from one form to another.
Compression: reducing data size while preserving important information.
Indexing: creating access paths for faster lookup.
Mapping: relating one data structure or meaning-space to another.
Classification: assigning data to categories.
Annotation: adding explanatory data onto existing data.
Ways Of Seeing Data

Perspective: a viewpoint that changes what is emphasized.
View: a selected or computed presentation of underlying data.
Lens: a rule or interpretive layer for looking at data.
Frame of reference: the coordinate system or assumptions used to interpret data.
Granularity: the level of detail in the data.
Resolution: how fine or coarse the data is.
Dimensionality: what axes/features the data is described through.
Salience: which data points stand out as important.
Relevance: which data matters for a given purpose.
Interpretation: assigning meaning to data.
Semantics: what the data means.
Syntax: how the data is formally arranged.
Boundaries And Relations

Boundary: where one data region/system/concept ends and another begins.
Interface: the surface through which data is accessed or exchanged.
Relation: a connection between data items.
Reference: a pointer from one piece of data to another.
Dependency: data that relies on other data.
Hierarchy: parent-child organization of data.
Topology: the shape of relationships among data.
Graph: data represented as nodes and edges.
Cluster: grouped data based on similarity or relation.
Truth, Meaning, And Uncertainty

Signal: meaningful data.
Noise: irrelevant or misleading data.
Bias: systematic distortion in data or interpretation.
Uncertainty: lack of complete confidence in data.
Ambiguity: data that can support multiple meanings.
Validity: whether data measures what it claims to measure.
Fidelity: how accurately data preserves the original.
Ground truth: the reference data treated as correct.
Inference: deriving new data or conclusions from existing data.
A tight “core set” around your examples would be:

abstraction, context, perspective, scope, frame, view, lens, schema, model, representation, granularity, projection, mapping, interpretation, semantics, provenance, boundary, relation, signal.

# # Concepts Similar to Abstraction, Perspective, Context

## Core Framing

These concepts are meta-concepts for shaping, selecting, transforming, organizing, or interpreting data.

They fall into four major groups:

1. Focal Data  
2. Surrounding Data  
3. Operations on Data  
4. Ways of Seeing Data

---

## Focal Data

### Content
The actual data being carried.

### Structure
The internal organization of the key data itself; the arrangement that makes it hold together.

### Form
The shape or medium the data takes.

### Pattern
Recurring structure across data.

### Relation
Connection between pieces of data.

### Hierarchy
Structure arranged by levels.

### Topology
Structure defined by connectivity or shape.

### Composition
How smaller units combine into larger ones.

### Configuration
A specific arrangement of parts.

### Layout
Spatial or presentational structure.

### Sequence
Order-based structure.

### Graph
Node-edge relational structure.

### Matrix
Grid or axis-based structure.

### Tree
Branching hierarchical structure.

---
